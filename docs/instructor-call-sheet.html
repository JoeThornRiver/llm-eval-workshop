Block A 0:00 15 min You present

A The question that costs you a day

Builds → why a human reads outputs at all

Open on the destination, not on LLM theory. Three beats: the customer's question, the scenario the audience has never seen, then the live demo. They must know by 0:05 what the session is for and how an ice cream counter works.

Say

"Your customer asks: we want to switch to model X, does your software still work? Today someone senior blocks out a day, re-reads outputs, and forms an opinion. Then does it again for the next model. That cost scales with models times cases, and it never goes down. By 1:35 today we will answer that question in twenty-four seconds, with a verdict you can put in writing. Everything between here and there exists to make that verdict defensible."

Set the scene first — 90 seconds, and do not skip it

The cold open fails without this

The audience has never seen this app. If they do not know that a scoop must sit in a container and that the model must never choose the container itself, the punchline of the demo is invisible — they will just see some JSON. Full version: The scenario at the top of the repo README.md.

Say

"The product is an ice cream cafe with a queue at the counter. Instead of a cashier tapping buttons, the customer just says it: two scoops of chocolate in a waffle cone with cream, please. Speech-to-text makes a transcript, the transcript and the menu go to an LLM, and the LLM returns a structured order that the rest of the software prices, shows on a screen, and sends to the counter. Nobody types. That model is the only thing between spoken language and a priced order."

Say

"Four things about the domain, and they are just how a counter works. Scoops — vanilla, chocolate — cannot be sold on their own; they need a container. Containers are the waffle cone and the cup; they are free and they hold the scoops. Standalone items — a sundae, a milkshake, a cappuccino, a Coke — are sold on their own and never go inside a container. And extras split in two: an option is single-select, one flavour and one milk; an add-on is stackable, cream and sauce, and only from that item's own list."

Say

"Now the one rule that produces most of what we will look at today. If the customer names scoops but no container, the model must not pick one. It keeps everything else, leaves the scoops out, and asks: cone or cup? Guessing is the failure you will see most often — and it is a real one, because the customer gets charged for a cone they never chose."

Show

Put the four worked examples from the README on screen — the fastest way to land all of it: two chocolate scoops in a cone; a scoop plus a sundae (scoop held back, question asked); a stracciatella sundae, which is one menu item and not a scoop in a cup; and a banana split, which is not on the menu at all.

Say

"So why does it take a human a day to check a new model? Because you cannot assert your way to the answer. Watch."

Show

The distinction to nail now — it pays off at 1:35

recorded is one model's output, a specimen captured once. expected is ground truth about the transcript, so it is true for any model — which is exactly why Tier 4 can reuse these same files to grade a candidate nobody has tested. And expectedFindings describes the defects in that recording; for a live model the expectation is simply zero findings.

Do not misread the count line — it will catch you live

- Expected - 4 and + Received + 1 are diff line counts, not numbers of findings. case-03 expects two findings, printed across four lines of array. Read "expected four" aloud and you will confuse the room for ten minutes.

The other shape of failure is the lesson

When the plus side has something the minus side does not, that is a false positive — a check fired on behaviour the case says is fine. Precision failures look different from recall failures in the diff, and both fail the suite. The one you will see most is case-05: a HALLUCINATED_ITEM check that forgets to skip names which do not resolve reports both codes where the case expects only UNRESOLVED_NAME.

Note

The four passing cases print nothing — you get nine failure blocks and then the summary. The summary is the progress meter, so for a quick check without scrolling: bun test evals/01-deterministic 2>&1 | tail -4.

Say

"And what does green mean when you get there? That on all thirteen cases your detected finding set matches the human-labelled one exactly — nothing missed, nothing invented. That is the Tier-1 contract. Hold on to it, because at 1:35 that same function is what grades a model nobody has tested before."

Say

"Two things to hold on to. First, the harness fails on false positives too — a check that fires on a healthy case is as broken as one that misses a defect, because a suite that cries wolf gets deleted. Second, and this is the one that matters today: these fixtures carry a recording of one model's output, but the golden labels next to them are model-independent. Written once, reusable against every candidate forever. For a live candidate the expectation is simply: zero findings."

Facilitate

Every fixture has a note field explaining why the case exists — point at it instead of answering. The four already-passing cases are case-01, case-02, case-12, case-13; case-02 passes only because SCHEMA_INVALID ships done.

The three traps that will eat the room

Trap 1 · the double-fire on case-05

"Choco Scoop" does not resolve, so it raises UNRESOLVED_NAME. If their HALLUCINATED_ITEM check does not skip names that fail to resolve, it fires too and the case fails on a false positive. Golden labels only apply to names that exist. Expect this within ten minutes.

Trap 2 · present-but-empty on case-04

That fixture has "scoops": []. Writing TYPE_FIELD_MISMATCH as !g.scoops?.length treats the empty array as missing and the case collects an extra finding. Type consistency tests presence; emptiness belongs to EMPTY_CONTAINER_GROUP.

Trap 3 · cases with two findings

case-03 expects TYPE_FIELD_MISMATCH and HALLUCINATED_ITEM — the spurious Cup is also an item nobody ordered. case-09 expects only INVALID_OPTION_OR_ADDON, because its clarification is present and correct.

Answer

solutions/checks.ts — the implementations; the .solution.test.ts beside it is only the assertion loop. It sits in solutions/ and not src/ deliberately: in src/ it would be the first place anyone looks. Note for later: Block F imports this exact file — the checks they just wrote are what grades candidate models.

Say

Closing discussion — the prompt that lands hardest: which of these defects would validateOrder have silently repaired at runtime, and why do we still want the eval to fail? Because a model that constantly needs rescuing is a quality problem you want to see, not hide. And when judging a new model, how often it needs rescuing is precisely what you are buying.

Block C 0:45 25 min They work · live API

C The judge for the residual

Builds → the half of the verdict that needs taste

Three beats: explain what this run actually grades (not the recordings), let them feel the useless rubric, then have them fix it. Nobody skips the middle one.

Say

"The gate tells you the output is well-formed, resolves, and obeys policy. It cannot tell you whether a clarification is helpful, or whether the model picked the reasonable reading of an ambiguous order. If we are going to rank vendors, we need a number for that. Here is the naive version."

Say this before you run it — the one thing everyone gets wrong

Tier 1 grades recordings · Tier 2 grades the live model

bun test evals/01-deterministic reads each fixture's recorded field — frozen output, planted defects. bun run eval:judge ignores recorded completely: for each of the 13 transcripts it calls the live model, then asks the judge to score that fresh output. Thirteen matching calls plus thirteen judge calls.

Say it explicitly, because the room will otherwise assume the judge is grading the same defective recordings they just spent thirty minutes on — and then every number confuses them. The planted defects are not in this run at all.

Mechanics

The judge prompt carries: the rubric, the menu, the transcript, the golden expectations (allowedItems, expectClarification) and the output to grade. The judge reasons step by step and must end with a line reading exactly SCORE: <n>, which the harness extracts by regex. No match → that case is an error and drops out of the average, which is what n= reports. The average is compared against PASS_THRESHOLD = 4.0 and becomes the exit code.

Pre-empt three arguments

Everyone's numbers differ — live output on both sides, every run. Nobody is doing it wrong.
The judge is not blind: it is handed the golden labels. That makes it more accurate and means it can never be used to validate those labels.
Sonnet is judging Haiku. Deliberate, and it is discussion question 3 — what happens to your eval when the model under test catches up with the judge?

Say

"Ten of thirteen scored a five. Two runs of this same command, same thirteen cases, came out at 4.62 and 4.54 — and case-09 moved from a 5 to a 3 on identical input. So the number is unstable, it is bunched at the top, and it passes. Would you gate a release on it? Would you tell a customer their new model is fine because of it?"

Now show them WHY it is a 5 — the moment that sells the exercise

Expect · read the middle sentence aloud

You get several paragraphs of appreciation. Notice what never happens: it does not check a single policy. It has opinions about tone and helpfulness, offers a "minor critique" that is actually a compliment, and awards full marks. That is what "rate this 1 to 5" buys you — fluent prose about vibes, wearing a number.

Say

"And be careful about the scores you cannot explain. case-12 came back a 3. Is that fair? The live model may well have invented a cone on that run — remember Block A, where it did exactly that nine times out of ten. With this rubric you cannot tell whether a 3 is deserved or noise, and neither can I. That is the actual problem: not that the number is wrong, but that it is unfalsifiable. Fixing that is the next thirty minutes, and measuring whether you fixed it is the block after."

Step 2

They rewrite RUBRIC in evals/02-judge/run-judge.ts — that constant is the only thing they touch; the harness is complete. Three constraints: concrete criteria ordered by importance, an anchor for every score level, at least one non-negotiable as a hard cap. Then re-run.

Tell them what "better" looks like, or they will chase the average. A good rubric usually makes the average go down, because it stops handing out fives. What you want is spread — bad cases scoring low — and the same case landing on the same score twice. Thirteen cases and one run cannot prove that by eye, which is exactly why the next block measures it instead of asserting it.

Block D 1:10 15 min You drive

D Trust the judge, or don't ship it

Builds → proof the judge's number means anything

The hinge of the session. A model verdict resting on an unvalidated judge is an opinion with extra steps — and this is the block that will be challenged, so lead with the measurement.

Say

"You just wrote a rubric you believe in. I believe in it too. Neither of us has measured it. So: take outputs a human has already scored, run the judge over them, and compare — agreement, ordering, and specifically whether it catches the cases the human failed. Watch what the useless rubric does."

bun run eval:calibrate -- --rubric placeholder --repeats 1
  Severe inversions (the rubric is missing a criterion the human used):
    case-12 (human +3 better than case-02) was scored LOWER by the judge
  VERDICT: NOT USABLE — do not gate a release on this judge yet
Why --repeats 1 here

The default is 3 repeats and takes ~2.5 minutes. One repeat runs in 30 seconds and still shows the inversions; it only forfeits the judge self-agreement figure. Say that out loud — it models the tradeoff honestly rather than hiding it.

Say

"An inversion is the diagnostic that matters. The judge ranked a policy-perfect output below an output that is not even parseable. A systematic offset you can fix by moving a threshold. An inversion means your rubric is missing a criterion the human is using — no threshold reaches that."

Define the words before you show the table

Nobody in the room signed up for inter-rater statistics. One sentence each for κ, severe recall and inversions, in that order, and the table lands. Speakable versions: The five numbers, said out loud.

"Three things. The rubric we teach beats the useless one on every metric — and still fails its own gate; 'better than useless' is not a standard. Ours are synthetic — derived from the rubric anchors plus each fixture's known defects. They prove the apparatus works and locate the judge's blind spots; they do not prove human agreement. Say so before someone finds it in the README. Then make the point that wins the room: their existing manual eval history is the label set that would make this real.

Define P95 before anyone squints at it

P95 means the 95th percentile of response time — line every call up from fastest to slowest and read off the point where 95% of them are quicker. So it is the slowest response a customer sees in roughly one call out of twenty. Say that sentence, not the word "percentile".

Why not the average? Because the average is the number that hides the problem. Haiku averages 1994 ms and its P95 is 5191 ms — so one order in twenty waits over five seconds at a counter with a queue behind it. Llama's tail is 1736 ms, three times better, on a model that is worse at the actual job. That trade is invisible in a mean, and it is exactly what an operations person will ask about.

Be straight about the sample if pressed: at 26 calls per model, P95 is the second-slowest call. A smoke signal, not a measurement — you would want hundreds of calls before quoting it to a customer.

κ plain words Reference

The five numbers, said out loud

Block D throws statistics at a room that did not ask for them. Define each one in a sentence before you show the table, in this order — each is a different way for a judge to be wrong, and each has a different fix.

First, the two Greek letters — someone will ask

κ is the letter kappa; ρ is the letter rho, pronounced to rhyme with "go". Neither is an abbreviation of anything — they are simply the conventional symbols for these two statistics, the way π is the symbol for a ratio nobody spells out. So "Spearman ρ" and "Spearman's rank correlation" are the same thing said two ways, and "weighted κ" is "Cohen's weighted kappa".

They are named after the people who proposed them: Charles Spearman, 1904, for the rank correlation; Jacob Cohen, 1960, for kappa. If you would rather not put Greek on a slide, write rank correlation and chance-corrected agreement — the words are the meaning, the letters are just shorthand.

Chance-corrected agreement weighted κ, say "kappa" — "agreement, minus the luck"

Say

"A judge that shouts five at everything will agree with our human on every case the human also scored five. That is luck, not skill. Kappa measures how much better than that guessing baseline you did: one is perfect, zero is no better than chance, and below zero is worse than chance. And ours is weighted, which means being off by four points counts sixteen times worse than being off by one — because the penalty grows with the square of the gap. Unweighted, 'human five judge one' would count the same as 'human five judge four', which is nonsense for a score."

If someone asks what a good κ is

The common rule of thumb reads 0.41–0.60 as "moderate" and 0.61–0.80 as "substantial" — a convention, not a law. Our gate is 0.60, deliberately unambitious: it says this judge is at least tracking the human, not this judge is good. Say that, rather than implying the number is a standard.

Rank correlation Spearman ρ, say "rho" — "same order?"

Say

"Rho — that is the Greek letter, just the name of the statistic — ignores the actual numbers and asks only whether the judge ranks the outputs the way the human does. Minus one is perfectly backwards, plus one is identical order. It is the forgiving cousin of kappa: a judge that is reliably one point too generous still ranks everything correctly, so rho stays high while kappa falls. When you see that gap, the judge is miscalibrated but still useful."

Average bias offset — "kind or harsh?"

Say

"Just the average of judge minus human. Positive means kinder. This is the cheap failure — if your judge is consistently a point generous, sharpen the anchors or move the threshold to match. Nothing about the rubric's logic is broken."

Failure catch-rate severe recall — "did it catch the disasters?"

Say

"Of the outputs our human called failures, how many did the judge also fail? Failure means a two or below, on both sides — six of our thirteen cases. It gets its own number because an average hides a catastrophe: a judge can look respectable overall and still wave through the one output that embarrasses you in front of a customer. This is plain recall — of the real problems, how many did you catch."

Opposite-ranked pairs inversions — "ranked the wrong way round"

Say

"Take any two outputs. The human says A is better than B; the judge says B is better than A. That pair is inverted. We call it severe when the human's gap was two points or more — not a close call. And this is the one that matters most, because of what it implies: an offset means the judge is miscalibrated, and a threshold absorbs that. An inversion means the judge is using different criteria than the human — something they care about is simply missing from your rubric. No threshold, no temperature setting and no bigger judge model fixes a wrong ordering. You have to go and write the missing criterion. Which is exactly what we did between version one and version two."

The example that makes it concrete

The placeholder rubric ranked case-12 — the output that follows the container policy perfectly — below case-02, which is not even parseable. Show that one pair and nobody needs the definition repeated.

Repeatability self-agreement — "does it agree with itself?"

Say

"Same judge, same rubric, same input, three times — how often does it give the identical score? This caps everything else. A judge that cannot agree with itself cannot agree with you, and no rewording rescues that. Pin the temperature first, then consider a stronger judge model."

Order

Q&A answers Measured

Questions you will get, with numbers from this repo

"Can't you set temperature to 0 and make it deterministic?"
The judge already runs at temperature 0 and still agrees with itself on only 92% of items. And determinism would not help: the Block A output is stable and wrong. You cannot assert your way out of a confidently repeated mistake.
"Why not let a model generate the golden labels? Labelling is expensive."
Then your eval measures agreement between two models, not correctness. It will look excellent and tell you nothing. Cheatsheet rule 4.
"Do we need the deterministic gate if we have a judge?"
case-02 is an output missing a required field — unparseable, useless to the consuming system. The judge scored it 5, three times running, because it grades content and never sees the schema. The gate catches it in a millisecond, free.
"Isn't the judge the expensive part?"
Yes, and by more than people expect: in the live Block F run the judging cost $0.0544 against $0.0008 for the model being judged — 68×. That asymmetry is the argument for tiering.
"Why an average of 4.0 rather than everything passing?"
A single flaky case must not redden the build, or the team learns to ignore red. The flip side, honestly: both our runs landed near 4.5, close enough that a bad day flips it. The fix is sharper anchors, never a lower threshold.
"How many golden cases do we need?"
Thirteen is a teaching set. For a gate you would defend to a customer: 50–100 labelled items, stratified across happy paths, known failure modes and adversarial input. The calibration run prints that caveat itself.
"We use MCP servers — does any of this apply?"
About two-thirds transfers: golden datasets, gate-first, judge, calibration, tiering. What changes is the unit under test — a trajectory rather than a single call — so the harness transfers as discipline, not as code. And most of the official MCP threat model is OAuth and application security, not model behaviour. Written up in evals/06-mcp/README.md.
"Could we just use a benchmark leaderboard instead?"
A leaderboard cannot tell you that a model is rejected by your schema, hallucinates items from your menu, or costs $3.50 per thousand of your orders. Row three of the comparison table is the answer to this question.
